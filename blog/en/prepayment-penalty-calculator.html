Prepayment Penalty in Germany — How the Active-Passive Method Works

15 May 2026 · 11 min read

If you pay off your German mortgage before the fixed-rate period ends, the bank charges a prepayment penalty — known as Vorfälligkeitsentschädigung (VFE). When selling a property, refinancing or after an inheritance, the bill can quickly run into five figures. Knowing how the German Federal Court of Justice (BGH) calculates the penalty lets you verify the bank's claim — and often negotiate it down by thousands of euros.

This article shows how the active-passive method from BGH ruling XI ZR 285/03 works, when you owe nothing at all, and how to estimate the penalty in under a minute with our prepayment penalty calculator.

What is the prepayment penalty?

The prepayment penalty is compensation a bank charges when you repay a fixed-rate loan early. The economic logic: the bank funded itself on the capital market — typically via Pfandbriefe (covered bonds) — at a fixed yield matching the loan's fixed-rate period. If you repay early, the bank can only reinvest the cash at today's prevailing market yield, usually lower. The bank may claim that interest gap as damages.

The legal basis is §490 (2) BGB (the borrower's right to extraordinary termination for legitimate reasons), combined with established BGH case law. The leading ruling — BGH XI ZR 285/03 — fixed the calculation method: the active-passive method.

The active-passive method explained

The active-passive method looks at the loan from two angles simultaneously:

  • Active side: Which interest payments would the bank still have received from you until the end of the fixed-rate period?
  • Passive side: What yield does the bank earn if it reinvests the repaid principal today in Pfandbriefe of matching residual maturity?

The difference between these two values — discounted to today's present value using the current reinvestment yield — equals the interest margin damage. Plus a processing fee and administrative overhead, that is your prepayment penalty.

VFE formula (simplified active-passive method)

VFE = interest margin damage + processing fee + administrative overhead

Interest margin damage ≈ outstanding balance × (nominal rate − reinvestment yield) / 12 × annuity factor

Annuity factor = (1 − (1 + i_w)^−n) / i_w, where i_w = reinvestment yield / 12 and n = remaining fixed-rate period in months.

Worked example: €150,000 balance, 36 months remaining

A common scenario: three years ago you took out a €200,000 annuity loan at 3.5%. Your outstanding balance today is €150,000. The fixed-rate period runs for another 3 years (36 months). You sell the property and redeem the loan. Today's 3-year Pfandbrief reinvestment yield is 2.2%.

  • Monthly margin loss = 150,000 × (3.5% − 2.2%) ÷ 12 = €162.50
  • i_w = 0.022 ÷ 12 = 0.001833
  • Annuity factor n=36 ≈ 34.86
  • Interest margin damage = 162.50 × 34.86 ≈ €5,665
  • + Processing fee €300 + administrative flat rate €30
  • VFE ≈ €5,995

When you owe no prepayment penalty

Four situations exempt you completely:

1. §489 BGB — termination after 10 years

Every borrower has a statutory special termination right: ten years after full disbursement of the loan you may terminate with six-month notice — penalty-free. This applies to all fixed-rate loans (§489 (1) No. 2 BGB) and cannot be contracted away.

2. Contractual special-repayment rights

If your contract allows, say, 5% annual special repayments, no penalty applies on that portion. Check your contract carefully — many German banks include 5% to 10% by default.

3. Defective prepayment clause (§502 BGB)

Banks must disclose transparently and completely how they calculate the VFE in the loan agreement. If this information is missing or insufficient, the entire claim falls away. The BGH confirmed this in 2018 (XI ZR 233/16) for consumer loans — more than half of all clauses reviewed were void.

4. Termination by the bank itself

If the bank cancels the loan (e.g. due to default), it usually cannot claim a penalty — it voluntarily ended the contract.

Tip: have the clause reviewed

Consumer protection organisations and banking-law specialists often review prepayment clauses at low cost. A void clause lets you refuse the penalty — or reclaim one already paid.

Common disputes about the calculation

Which reinvestment yield is correct?

Banks like to apply the lowest available Pfandbrief yield — the lower the reinvestment, the higher the margin damage. The correct value is the average Pfandbrief yield for the exact residual maturity, published in the Bundesbank PEX index. A 0.2% difference can move the penalty by several hundred euros.

How is scheduled amortization handled?

With an annuity loan you repay principal every month. The bank must reflect this in its calculation — each month the outstanding balance falls, so does the lost interest. Anyone computing only on the starting balance overpays.

Special-repayment rights matter

If your contract grants you 5% annual special repayments, the bank must assume you would have used that right. The penalty can only apply to the portion that could not have been covered by special repayments.

Negotiation strategy: reducing the VFE

Banks usually leave room in their favour in the first VFE quote. Three realistic levers:

  1. Submit your own calculation: If your figure is more than €500 below the bank's, demand a written breakdown. Precise comparison shows the bank you understand the procedure.
  2. Have the clause reviewed: For loans signed since 2010 many clauses are void. A banking lawyer or consumer protection agency (around €50 flat fee) usually pays off.
  3. Negotiate, don't fight: Once the bank knows you could escalate, a 10% to 20% discount is often on the table — especially for long-standing clients.

When does early repayment make sense?

Not every early redemption is economically sensible. A quick rule of thumb:

  • Refinancing: Worthwhile if the interest savings on the new loan over the remaining fixed-rate period exceed VFE plus all fees.
  • Property sale: The penalty is usually unavoidable but can be voided if the buyer takes over the loan (rarely feasible).
  • Inheritance / windfall: Anyone redeeming the entire balance should compare carefully — investing the cash at 4–7% returns is often more attractive than redeeming a 2.5% loan.

FAQ on the prepayment penalty

Can the bank set the VFE arbitrarily?

No. The method is fixed by BGH case law. Banks must explain the calculation transparently. You are entitled to a written breakdown.

What is a flat-rate penalty?

For consumer loans without a mortgage charge, §502 BGB caps the penalty at 1% of the outstanding balance (0.5% if less than 12 months remain). Real-estate loans secured by a Grundschuld are not subject to this cap.

How quickly must the bank deliver the calculation?

On request, the bank must provide a calculation for the planned redemption date promptly — typically within 1 to 2 weeks. The figure is normally valid for two weeks.

Can I sue against an excessive VFE?

Yes. If the bank uses inflated parameters or the prepayment clause is void, you can sue at the regional court (Landgericht, claim amount usually above €5,000). Chances of success are very high for consumer loans signed since 2010.

Bottom line

The prepayment penalty is calculable — and therefore negotiable. The key takeaways:

  1. The BGH active-passive method (XI ZR 285/03) is binding for every bank.
  2. After 10 years of running time you can terminate under §489 BGB penalty-free.
  3. Processing fees above €300 are usually inadmissible.
  4. A void prepayment clause kills the entire claim.
  5. Your own calculation plus clause review can save thousands of euros.

The VFE is not a verdict — it is a calculation with room to negotiate.
